6 Blender modeling exercises with a graded rubric

Six exercises in order of difficulty: a mug, a rounded cube with support loops, a chair to real dimensions, a sci-fi crate, a sword and a tree. Each lists the target, the budget and what "good" looks like, and every rubric is free to read. Export OBJ, drop it in, and the page counts your n-gons, poles and non-manifold edges before any review. A full AI review with a grade is $9.

  1. Low-poly mug: a body with wall thickness, a rim and a handle in 800 faces, no n-gons. Teaches spinning a profile, extruding and joining a handle cleanly.
  2. Rounded cube, holding edges: a subdivision cube whose edges stay crisp through support loops. Teaches how edge spacing sets the radius of a subdivided corner.
  3. Chair to dimensions: a dining chair with a 45 cm seat height from an orthographic brief. Teaches working to real-world scale and units.
  4. Sci-fi crate: panels, bevels and inset details on a box. Teaches hard-surface forms that shade cleanly.
  5. Low-poly sword: a game asset under 1,500 triangles. Teaches spending polygons where the silhouette needs them.
  6. Low-poly tree: a stylized tree under 500 triangles. Teaches reading form at a distance with very few faces.

How each exercise is graded

Every challenge publishes a rubric with weights that add up to 100. "Topology, measured" is scored by code from your OBJ, free, the moment you drop the file: face budget, n-gons, quad share, poles, non-manifold and open edges, and size. The rest (silhouette and proportion, brief compliance, shading artefacts) is graded by AI in the full review from seven snapshots your browser renders. Edge flow is noted but not scored. The pass bar is B (80).

Before you start

Work at real scale (Blender's default unit is metres, so a 10 cm mug is 0.1 units tall), keep modifiers live while you work, and export OBJ with Triangulate Faces off. The export guide has the steps for Blender, Maya and 3ds Max.

Start with the low-poly mug or check any OBJ in the free topology checker.

Reviews are written by AI (Claude, by Anthropic) against each challenge's published rubric; topology counts are measured by code.
